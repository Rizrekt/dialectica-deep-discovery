### 1. Introduction and Orbital/Temporal Framework for 2028

The surface atmospheric pressure of Mars is fundamentally governed by the seasonal exchange of carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$) between the atmosphere and the polar ice reservoirs. Because $\text{CO}_2$ constitutes approximately 95% of the Martian atmospheric inventory by volume, the condensation and sublimation of the seasonal polar caps causes a planetary atmospheric mass variation of 25% to 30% over each Martian year (Hess et al., 1980; Tillman, 1988).

To project pressure readings at Chryse Planitia ($22.48^\circ\text{ N}, 312.05^\circ\text{ E}$, elevation $\approx -1.5\text{ km}$ relative to the MOLA aeroid datum; Smith et al., 2001) during the Earth calendar year 2028, the planetary orbital position must be fixed via the solar longitude of Mars ($L_s$):
- **Martian Year (MY) 39**: Begins at the northern spring equinox ($L_s = 0^\circ$) on September 30, 2026. The southern summer solstice ($L_s = 270^\circ$, northern winter solstice) occurs on **March 18, 2028**.
- **Martian Year 40**: Begins at $L_s = 0^\circ$ on August 17, 2028. The northern summer solstice ($L_s = 90^\circ$, southern winter solstice) occurs in the subsequent Earth year on **February 3, 2029**.

Because the Question specifies the **Martian summer solstice of 2028**, it refers astronomically either to the **Southern Summer Solstice of MY 39** occurring in Earth year 2028 ($L_s = 270^\circ$, March 2028), or to the **Northern Summer Solstice** ($L_s = 90^\circ$, which governs local Chryse summer, occurring in April 2027 for MY 39 and February 2029 for MY 40). Both orbital regimes are synthesized below, detailing the respective sublimation-condensation states and their effect on Chryse Planitia barometric peaks.

### 2. Historical Viking 1 Pressure Baseline and Climatology

Viking Lander 1 (VL-1) recorded continuous in situ surface pressure at Chryse Planitia from landing at $L_s = 96.5^\circ$ in July 1976 through late 1982 (spanning over three complete Martian years; Hess et al., 1977; Tillman et al., 1993). The daily pressure signal $P(t)$ decomposes into two primary components:
1. **The seasonal background mean pressure $\bar{P}(L_s)$**, driven by the planetary $\text{CO}_2$ condensation-sublimation mass budget and global atmospheric dynamics.
2. **The diurnal tidal oscillation $\Delta P_{\text{tide}}(t)$**, driven by solar thermal forcing of the atmosphere and suspended dust, generating migrating diurnal ($S_1$) and semi-diurnal ($S_2$) pressure waves (Leovy & Zurek, 1979; Zurek, 1980).

The peak daily pressure reading for any given sol is:
$$P_{\text{peak}} = \bar{P}(L_s) + \Delta P_{\text{peak\_tide}}(L_s, \tau)$$
where $\tau$ is the column dust optical depth.

#### Historical Viking 1 Mean Barometric Features:
- **Global Annual Minimum 1 ($L_s \approx 140^\circ - 150^\circ$)**: As the south polar winter cap condenses under unilluminated conditions during aphelion season, planetary atmospheric mass reaches an annual minimum. $\bar{P} \approx 6.75 - 6.90\text{ hPa}$ (Hess et al., 1980; Hourdin et al., 1993).
- **Secondary Maximum ($L_s \approx 60^\circ - 90^\circ$, Northern Summer)**: Sublimation of the northern seasonal $\text{CO}_2$ cap releases mass back into the atmosphere, causing $\bar{P}$ to rise to an inflection plateau of $\approx 7.80 - 8.10\text{ hPa}$ near $L_s = 70^\circ - 90^\circ$ (Tillman et al., 1993).
- **Global Annual Maximum ($L_s \approx 270^\circ - 280^\circ$, Southern Summer Solstice / Perihelion)**: Due to Mars’ orbital eccentricity ($e \approx 0.0934$), perihelion occurs at $L_s \approx 251^\circ$. The southern polar cap, which accumulates approximately $1.5 \times 10^{12}\text{ metric tons}$ of seasonal dry ice during southern winter, is subjected to maximal solar irradiance ($S \approx 715\text{ W/m}^2$, compared to $S \approx 490\text{ W/m}^2$ at aphelion; Wood & Paige, 1992). The southern cap sublimates rapidly, driving global atmospheric mass to its annual maximum, with baseline $\bar{P} \approx 8.90 - 9.20\text{ hPa}$ at Chryse Planitia under clear conditions, and exceeding $10.0\text{ hPa}$ during global dust storms (Hess et al., 1977; Ryan & Henry, 1979).

### 3. Physical Model Formulation

#### 3.1. Polar Cap Sublimation Mass Balance
The local rate of $\text{CO}_2$ phase change per unit area of the polar cap, $\dot{m}_{\text{CO}_2}(\phi, t)$, is governed by the surface energy balance over the permanent and seasonal ice sheets:
$$(1 - A_{\text{ice}}) F_{\odot}(\phi, L_s) \cos \theta_0 + F_{\text{IR}\downarrow} - \epsilon \sigma T_{\text{sub}}^2 = L_{\text{sub}} \dot{m}_{\text{CO}_2}$$
where:
- $A_{\text{ice}}$ is the seasonal cap albedo ($\sim 0.60 - 0.75$ for clean $\text{CO}_2$ snow, decreasing to $\sim 0.45 - 0.55$ when contaminated by dust; Kieffer et al., 2000),
- $F_{\odot}(\phi, L_s)$ is the top-of-atmosphere solar flux at latitude $\phi$ and orbital position $L_s$,
- $F_{\text{IR}\downarrow}$ is downwelling atmospheric infrared thermal radiation,
- $\epsilon \sigma T_{\text{sub}}^4$ is the surface radiative emission at the $\text{CO}_2$ saturation temperature ($T_{\text{sub}} \approx 145 - 150\text{ K}$ at ambient Martian pressures; Wood & Paige, 1992),
- $L_{\text{sub}} \approx 5.9 \times 10^5\text{ J/kg}$ is the latent heat of sublimation of $\text{CO}_2$.

Integrating $\dot{m}_{\text{CO}_2}$ over the illuminated polar cap areas $A_{\text{north}}$ and $A_{\text{south}}$ yields the net rate of global atmospheric mass change:
$$\frac{dM_{\text{atm}}}{dt} = \int_{A_{\text{south}}} \dot{m}_{\text{CO}_2}\, dA + \int_{A_{\text{north}}} \dot{m}_{\text{CO}_2}\, dA$$

By hydrostatic equilibrium, the global mean surface pressure $\langle P_s \rangle$ satisfies:
$$\langle P_s \rangle = \frac{M_{\text{atm}} g}{4 \pi R_{\text{Mars}}^2}$$
where $g \approx 3.72\text{ m/s}^2$ and $R_{\text{Mars}} \approx 3389.5\text{ km}$.

#### 3.2. Topographic and Dynamical Partitioning at Chryse Planitia
The mean surface pressure at Chryse Planitia ($\phi = 22.48^\circ\text{ N}$, elevation $z_0 \approx -1.5\text{ km}$) differs from the global mean $\langle P_s \rangle$ due to hypsometric compression and planetary-scale Hadley circulation:
$$\bar{P}_{\text{Chryse}}(L_s) = \langle P_s \rangle(L_s) \cdot \exp\left( -\frac{z_0 - \bar{z}}{\mathcal{H}} \right) + \Delta P_{\text{dyn}}(L_s)$$
where $\mathcal{H} = \frac{R_{\text{spec}} \bar{T}}{g} \approx 10.0 - 11.5\text{ km}$ is the atmospheric pressure scale height (with $R_{\text{spec}} \approx 189\text{ J}/(\text{kg}\cdot\text{K})$ for $\text{CO}_2$), and $\Delta P_{\text{dyn}}$ accounts for the seasonal cross-equatorial mass redistribution resulting from the solstitial Hadley cell (Hourdin et al., 1993; Forget et al., 1999).

#### 3.3. Diurnal Tidal Wave Superposition
The diurnal pressure variation $\Delta P(t)$ is represented by the sum of diurnal ($k=1$) and semi-diurnal ($k=2$) harmonic thermal tides:
$$\Delta P(t) = A_1(L_s, \tau) \cos\left( \frac{2\pi t}{24.66} - \phi_1 \right) + A_2(L_s, \tau) \cos\left( \frac{4\pi t}{24.66} - \phi_2 \right)$$
where $t$ is local true solar time (LTST) in hours.

Based on Viking 1 lander meteorology records (Leovy & Zurek, 1979; Wilson & Hamilton, 1996):
- In clear conditions ($\tau \approx 0.2 - 0.5$), the semi-diurnal tide $S_2$ dominates at low-to-mid latitudes because the diurnal mode $S_1$ is largely vertically trapped, whereas $S_2$ propagates freely. The tidal amplitudes are $A_1 \approx 0.05 - 0.12\text{ hPa}$ and $A_2 \approx 0.10 - 0.18\text{ hPa}$.
- Constructive interference between the morning rising phase of $S_1$ and $S_2$ causes the maximum diurnal pressure spike $\Delta P_{\text{peak\_tide}}$ to occur reliably between **08:30 and 10:00 LTST**, adding $+0.15\text{ hPa}$ to $+0.28\text{ hPa}$ above $\bar{P}$.
- Under dusty conditions ($\tau > 1.5$, characteristic of perihelion/southern summer), solar absorption by airborne dust amplifies the diurnal tide substantially, increasing $\Delta P_{\text{peak\_tide}}$ to $+0.40 - 0.85\text{ hPa}$ (Ryan & Henry, 1979; Zurek, 1980).

### 4. Quantitative Synthesis for 2028 Solstitial Scenarios

#### Scenario A: Southern Summer Solstice in Earth Year 2028 ($L_s = 270^\circ$, March 18, 2028)
*Physical State*:
- **Polar Sublimation**: Maximal. The vast southern seasonal cap has undergone intense insolation near perihelion ($L_s = 251^\circ$), releasing its primary reservoir of solid $\text{CO}_2$ into the global atmosphere. Sublimation has peaked and is completing, while condensation over the polar northern night is in its early stages.
- **Atmospheric Mass Balance**: Global mass $M_{\text{atm}}$ is at or near its global annual maximum.
- **Historical Viking 1 Baseline at $L_s = 270^\circ$**:
  - Unperturbed/Clear year (Viking Year 2 / 3 baseline): Daily mean pressure $\bar{P} \approx 8.95 \pm 0.15\text{ hPa}$ (Tillman et al., 1993).
  - Dust Storm Year (Viking Year 1, 1977b global storm): Daily mean pressure $\bar{P} \approx 9.80 - 10.20\text{ hPa}$ due to intense atmospheric warming and thermal expansion driving mass into the Chryse basin (Hess et al., 1977).
- **Diurnal Tidal Peak Offset**:
  - Clear atmosphere ($\tau \approx 0.3 - 0.5$): $\Delta P_{\text{peak\_tide}} \approx +0.22 \pm 0.05\text{ hPa}$. Peak occurs at $\sim 09: 15\text{ LTST}$.
  - Moderate-to-high dust opacity ($\tau \approx 1.0 - 2.0$): $\Delta P_{\text{peak\_tide}} \approx +0.45 \pm 0.15\text{ hPa}$.
- **Projected Peak Daily Reading**:
  - *Nominal (Quiescent / Moderate Dust)*: **$9.20\text{ hPa}$ to $9.45\text{ hPa}$** (confidence interval: $9.05 - 9.60\text{ hPa}$).
  - *Active Global Dust Event*: **$9.90\text{ hPa}$ to $10.60\text{ hPa}$**.

#### Scenario B: Northern Summer Solstice ($L_s = 90^\circ$, Local Chryse Summer)
*Physical State*:
- **Polar Sublimation**: Northern seasonal $\text{CO}_2$ cap has completed sublimation, exposing the residual water-ice cap. Meanwhile, the southern polar night is experiencing condensation of $\text{CO}_2$ into the southern seasonal cap. Because the southern condensation sink exceeds the residual northern release, global pressure is decreasing toward the annual aphelion minimum.
- **Historical Viking 1 Baseline at $L_s = 90^\circ$**:
  - Diurnal mean pressure $\bar{P} \approx 7.85 \pm 0.10\text{ hPa}$ (Tillman et al., 1993).
- **Diurnal Tidal Peak Offset**:
  - Northern summer occurs near aphelion; the atmosphere is cold, background dust optical depth is historically minimal ($\tau \approx 0.15 - 0.30$), and tidal amplitudes are stable and small: $\Delta P_{\text{peak\_tide}} \approx +0.16 \pm 0.04\text{ hPa}$.
- **Projected Peak Daily Reading**:
  - Peak occurs consistently between **08:45 and 09:30 LTST** at **$8.01 \pm 0.12\text{ hPa}$** (range: **$7.90\text{ hPa}$ to $8.15\text{ hPa}$**).

### 5. Uncertainty Quantification and Model Sensitivities

In accordance with empirical observations from Viking, Mars Global Surveyor, and Mars Science Laboratory (Forget et al., 1999; Haberle et al., 2014):

1. **Dust Optical Depth Coupling ($\tau$)**:
   - The largest single source of variance in the peak daily pressure reading during the southern summer solstice ($L_s = 270^\circ$, March 2028) is the stochastic occurrence of regional or planet-encircling dust storms (PEDS). A major storm increases peak daily readings by $0.8\text{ hPa}$ to $1.4\text{ hPa}$ through a combination of enhanced downwelling IR flux accelerating polar cap sublimation margins and tidal amplitude amplification. For the northern summer solstice ($L_s = 90^\circ$), dust storm probability is empirically $<1\%$, yielding low uncertainty ($\sigma < 0.15\text{ hPa}$).
2. **Cap Albedo and Asymmetry**:
   - The asymmetry in seasonal cap capacity (the southern cap holds roughly $60\%$ more $\text{CO}_2$ mass than the northern cap due to the longer, colder southern winter at aphelion; Wood & Paige, 1992) produces an asymmetric seasonal response. Peak daily pressure at Chryse is approximately $1.2\text{ hPa}$ to $1.5\text{ hPa}$ higher during the southern summer solstice than during the northern summer solstice.
3. **Interannual Basal Invariance**:
   - Analysis of multi-decadal Martian lander records (Viking 1, Viking 2, Phoenix, Curiosity, and InSight) demonstrates that the unperturbed non-dust baseline pressure curve at equivalent $L_s$ repeats within $\pm 0.05 - 0.10\text{ hPa}$ across decades, confirming that the total exchangeable $\text{CO}_2$ volatile inventory has remained strictly stationary over modern observation baselines (Tillman et al., 1993; Haberle et al., 2014).
