You are a strategy/systems advisor for a small operator running an Expert agent on Dialectica, a verified-knowledge marketplace. Answer the four decision questions at the end directly and specifically, citing the numbers given. For each: pick one direction, give the strongest reason, and name one piece of evidence that would overturn your verdict. No generic advice.

STRUCTURAL PROBLEM (measured facts from one 75-minute production flight):

An Expert answers questions in an adversarial Q&A arena. Its entry strategy ("doctrine") decides the entry mode PER QUESTION from the answer-arrival rate in the question's first 2 minutes of life: a quiet question -> enter fast (the "first verified answer" cell pays the most); a flooded question -> PARK it and enter late at age 2-6 hours (a periodic selection channel raises verdict probability from ~0.17-0.25 to ~0.50); a delay of 2-120 minutes at positions 11-60 on a flooded question is a forbidden dead zone.

Two pipelines ran side by side for 75 minutes:
- An OBSERVER classified 117 questions from the discovery feed (young questions, no keyword filter) and parked 113 late-entry tickets.
- A background SERVER (poller) took its work from its own built-in search: a narrow topic keyword + a 3-day age window, a 20-row result page, at most 3 rows attempted per round, server relevance order.

Result: the intersection of the two pools was ZERO. The server presented 12 questions — all unclassified (old/crowded, outside the observer's window) — burned 9 of the 10 daily generation allowances on drafts that quality gates then held, and submitted 0 answers. The 113 parked tickets never released, because the only bridge between the pools is a "prepare" step inside the server, and that step can only FILTER rows the server already picked — it can never PULL a ticket from the doctrine pool. Worse: for unclassified rows, the prepare step by design lets them flow into the legacy pipeline ("unknown -> treat as usual"), so even an armed doctrine did not change behavior on the rows that actually arrived.

Two tail findings from the same flight:
(a) A veto that fired one claim too late. One question was picked in its most valuable state (no verified answer yet) while its answer crowd was ALREADY 79 — a fact recorded in the pick data at selection time. A draft was generated, the question was CLAIMED, and then the send-time freshness veto held it. This post-claim rejection floats a claim artifact on the server and locks the Expert's single concurrency slot for ~30 minutes (a dropped job is released only by a server liveness timeout; there is no release API on the client side), rejecting the next claim ("at capacity (1/1)").
(b) The price of a single slot. Expert concurrency = 1, so every post-claim drop costs ~30 minutes of paralysis plus a reliability cost for an abandoned job. Preventing a claim that would be dropped is worth far more than holding its draft.

After the flight, three queries were measured read-only against the same classification store (117 classified, 113 parked, 70 due at that moment): the server's current query -> due-ticket intersection 1 (incidental); an age-band query of 2-6 hours with no keyword -> 70 of 70 due tickets present in its results (20 of 20 on the default 20-row page).

DESIGN OPTIONS:
Option A — Align the server to the doctrine band (already built, behind a flag, reversible). The server's search is replaced with the same age band as the ticket release window (age 2-6 hours, no keyword); the prepare step becomes fail-closed (an unclassified row while the doctrine is armed = skipped at zero cost — inside the band, a legitimate row has had >=2 hours to be classified by an observer polling every 45 seconds); the same freshness-veto rule is additionally evaluated ONCE at pick time as a pre-claim screen (the send-time veto stays, for room that fills during drafting). Consequence: the fast lane (<2h) is no longer served by the poller — fast slots lapse unused, consistent with a doctrine that never forces fast slots; the late lane (7 of the doctrine's 10 daily allowances) is structurally covered.
Option B — A ticket driver on top of targeted claims. A driver process watches the queue: a due ticket / a fast classification -> a targeted claim for exactly that question. Exact intersection for both lanes by construction. But the targeted-claim path does not pass through the prepare hook (direct claim), drafting happens at job time through a different provider path, and the freshness veto is only wired into the server's provider — adopting it means re-wiring proven gates onto a different pipeline (largest regression risk, largest build), and a session lock makes the driver REPLACE the poller, not accompany it.
Option C — Fail-closed prepare only. Stops the generation burn (9 -> 0 wasted) but does not close the disconnect: the server's page stays keyword-bound (measured due intersection = 1, incidental). Not sufficient alone.

QUESTIONS:
Q1 (structure): For a two-lane entry doctrine (late 2-6h as the backbone + a small fast lane), is aligning the single poller to the late band — and letting the fast lane lapse — the right structural call? Or is the fast lane valuable enough (the first-verified cell is the richest measured cell, 0.88-0.93) that a targeted mechanism like Option B must be built from the start, not later?
Q2 (fail-closed): While the doctrine is armed, unclassified rows are skipped — but the client imposes a 6-hour skip on rows skipped this session, so a ticket the observer classifies a few minutes later can still be missed until its window closes. Is fail-closed worth that classification-lag loss, versus a measured fail-open (e.g. unclassified rows may flow only while the day's generation allowance is above a safe threshold)?
Q3 (pre-claim screen): Evaluating the same veto rule twice (at pick as a screen, at send as a veto) — is there a second-order risk we are missing (divergent verdicts between the two evaluations over changing state, extra API read load per row, or a false sense that the screen replaces the veto)?
Q4 (single slot): With concurrency 1 and a post-claim drop costing ~30 minutes with no release API, is the pre-claim screen sufficient mitigation — or must claim sequencing also be explicitly conservative (e.g. never claim while the previous job's outcome is not yet certainly known)?
